What interviewers actually look for

Patterns distilled from 150+ real experiences · 2020–2025

01
Always state brute force first, then optimize
Every FAANG company expects you to verbalize the naive O(n²) solution before optimizing. Jumping straight to clever tricks without narrating the journey is scored negatively at Google. It shows you understand the problem, not just memorized the solution.
02
Name the pattern immediately — "this is a sliding window problem"
Meta E5 expects pattern identification within 2 minutes. Google interviewers use the pattern name as a springboard for follow-up escalation. Saying it out loud buys time and signals experience.
03
Google: narrate uncertainty — never go silent
Follow-up escalation is a core Google mechanic. When the problem changes, keep narrating: "The heap breaks here because… let me think whether a BST changes that." Structured uncertainty spoken aloud scores higher than silent certainty that never arrives. Request hints proactively — it's not penalized.
04
Meta: two problems in 35 minutes — it's a sprint
If problem 1 hits 18 minutes with no complete solution, submit brute force, state complexity, and pivot to problem 2. Partial credit on two problems always beats a perfect solution on one. The recruiter gives you a mock interview before onsite — use it.
05
Amazon: Leadership Principles are half the interview
LP and DSA are weighted equally. Prepare 8+ STAR stories with specific metrics ("reduced latency by 40%"). Customer Obsession, Deliver Results, Invent & Simplify, Dive Deep, Have Backbone cover 80% of LP asks. The Bar Raiser often asks zero DSA.
06
Apple: code quality is explicitly graded
Apple scores variable naming, modularity, and error handling separately from correctness. A working solution with poor hygiene can still fail. Write as if it's going into a real PR. Domain knowledge (iOS runtime, memory model) is tested alongside DSA.
07
Always proactively state complexity — don't wait to be asked
As soon as you finish coding: "This is O(n log k) time and O(k) space because…". Google and Netflix interviewers may interrupt mid-code to ask. Build the habit of annotating complexity as you write each loop or recursive call.
08
Amazon OA debugging round: train for it separately
The Amazon OA includes a debugging section almost nobody prepares for. Introduce bugs into your own past solutions, let them sit overnight, then debug them cold. This is a distinct skill from writing code from scratch.
09
Microsoft: hints are not penalties — use them actively
Microsoft interviewers drop hints by design to test whether you can translate a nudge into a solution. Accepting a hint and running with it scores well. Problem families (stock series, subarray, intervals) are escalated within a single round — know the full family.
10
Netflix: research the specific team before your loop
Netflix rounds are designed by the actual team you'd join — not a central committee. Read the team's engineering blog. Problems often map directly to real Netflix systems. Concurrency problems appear heavily on infra teams. Speed is scored at Netflix — finish with 10 minutes remaining.
